Abstract

This report presents a computational model that predicts the ascent trajectory of a single-stage launch vehicle and a global optimiser that searches the design space for the configuration reaching the highest altitude under fixed resource limits. The simulation is a 2-D point-mass model including vehicle mass variation, propellant consumption, thrust, drag, altitude-dependent gravity, atmospheric density (US Standard Atmosphere 1976), velocity, acceleration, altitude and time of flight. Six design variables (thrust, propellant mass, burn time, launch angle, drag coefficient and dry mass) are optimised with Differential Evolution followed by SLSQP refinement.

Relative to the baseline, the optimised vehicle raises maximum altitude from 9.28 km to 175.11 km and maximum velocity from 330 m/s to 1,639 m/s, while satisfying all propellant, mass, thrust, burn-time and launch-angle constraints. A Monte Carlo test (5 % parameter scatter) quantifies robustness.

Authors: Gur Asees, Anjandeep, Kairav

1. Problem statement

A launch vehicle carries a limited propellant mass and has a defined configuration. The task is to find design or operating parameters that maximise a performance objective (maximum altitude, maximum downrange distance, or minimum propellant requirement) subject to engineering constraints.

The simulation must model vehicle mass variation, propellant consumption, thrust, drag, gravity, atmospheric density, velocity, acceleration, altitude and time of flight. It must produce altitude, velocity, acceleration and mass versus time, maximum altitude, maximum velocity, time to apogee, and a baseline-versus-optimised comparison. The Advanced Level requires an algorithm that searches the design space automatically.

2. Mathematical model

The vehicle is a point mass in the vertical (downrange-altitude) plane. State: downrange x, altitude h, velocities vx, vy, and mass m.

Thrust and mass flow

Tx = T cos(θ)   Ty = T sin(θ)   (θ constant)
mdot = T / (Isp · g0)   m(t) = m_dry + m_prop(t)
T = 0 when t ≥ t_burn or m_prop ≤ 0

Burnout occurs at the earlier of the commanded burn time and propellant exhaustion. Unburned propellant stays on board as dead mass.

Drag, dynamic pressure, gravity

D = ½ ρ v² Cd A   Dx = −D·vx/v   Dy = −D·vy/v
q = ½ ρ v²   (Max-Q is the peak of q)
g(h) = g0 (R / (R + h))²   g0 = 9.80665, R = 6371 km

Equations of motion

dx/dt = vx   dh/dt = vy
dvx/dt = (Tx + Dx) / m
dvy/dt = (Ty + Dy) / m − g(h)
dm/dt = −mdot

Atmosphere

Density follows the US Standard Atmosphere 1976: piecewise layers with a hydrostatic relation and a lapse rate in each layer (ρ = p / (R_air·T)), valid to 86 km, with exponential decay above. A simple exponential model ρ = ρ0·exp(−h/H) is also available through the configuration.

3. Numerical method and software design

The equations are integrated with adaptive Runge-Kutta 4(5) (scipy.integrate.solve_ivp, rtol 1e-6, atol 1e-8, output every 0.05 s). Terminal events stop the run at ground impact (after liftoff) or at a safety altitude ceiling. A forward-Euler integrator is available for comparison. The optimiser uses looser tolerances (rtol 1e-4); the final designs are re-simulated with production settings so the comparison is fair.

5. Optimisation problem

maximise h_max(x), x = [T, m_prop, t_burn, θ, Cd, m_dry]
subject to m_prop ≤ 260 kg   m_dry + m_prop ≤ 450 kg
3,500 ≤ T ≤ 14,000 N   15 ≤ t_burn ≤ 90 s
55° ≤ θ ≤ 90°   0.22 ≤ Cd ≤ 0.55   130 ≤ m_dry ≤ 220 kg

The code minimises J = −h_max. Constraint violations receive a large linear penalty. Maximum downrange and minimum propellant to reach a target altitude are available as other objectives on the command line.

Stage 1, Differential Evolution: best1bin, population factor 9, 18 generations, mutation 0.5-1.0, recombination 0.7, seed 42. It is a global search that is not easily trapped in local optima.

Stage 2, SLSQP: local gradient-based refinement with an explicit initial-mass constraint. Total cost: 1,163 simulation evaluations.

7. Discussion

  • Why the altitude gain is so large. Apogee grows roughly with the square of burnout velocity. The mass ratio rises from 340/232 ≈ 1.47 (108 kg burned) to 387.7/130.1 ≈ 2.98 (all propellant burned), raising ideal Δv from about 920 to 2,630 m/s (Isp 245 s).
  • Use all the propellant. Propellant goes to the cap and dry mass to its lower bound. Unburned propellant is dead mass; the baseline leaves 71.6 kg unused.
  • Near-vertical launch. With a constant thrust direction, any horizontal component wastes energy against gravity. The optimum is 88.98°, which still leaves about 14 km of downrange drift.
  • Low drag and low structure mass. Cd and dry mass enter the losses almost linearly, so both hit their lower bounds. In a real design those bounds would set the answer.
  • Thrust is a genuine trade-off. Higher thrust cuts gravity loss but raises drag loss and Max-Q. The optimum (10.7 kN) is interior to 3.5-14 kN. Initial T/W rises from 1.86 to about 2.82.
  • Burn time is slack. The commanded 62.02 s is not binding because the engine stops at 57.65 s when propellant ends (mass flow 4.47 kg/s).

8. Verification and sensitivity

The code self-checks mass conservation, non-negative propellant, drag direction and thrust cut-off. Apogee is the interpolated zero crossing of vertical velocity and agrees with the maximum-altitude sample. The optimised design uses the same physics and integrator as the baseline.

Monte Carlo robustness. Forty samples with all six variables scattered at 5 % standard deviation gave a mean apogee of 173.7 km, a standard deviation of 19.6 km (about 11 %), and a range of 138.4-209.3 km. A 5 % input uncertainty therefore produces about 11 % output scatter, so apogee is highly sensitive to thrust, mass and angle tolerances. Scattered samples may fall slightly outside the bounds because no re-clipping is applied. Try it in the Monte Carlo tab above.

9. Limitations

  • Single stage, constant thrust magnitude and direction (no gravity turn or pitch programme).
  • Point mass: no rotation, aerodynamic moments, wind or Earth rotation; flat downrange.
  • Constant Isp (no nozzle altitude compensation) and constant Cd (no Mach dependence; transonic drag rise ignored).
  • No structural, thermal or g-load limits beyond the set bounds. The optimum shows Max-Q ≈ 295 kPa and ≈ 8.3 g peak acceleration.
  • Differential Evolution ran only 18 generations and stopped on the iteration limit, not convergence. More generations or seeds would confirm the global optimum.
  • The model is educational and does not represent any operational vehicle; baseline values are assumed.

10. Conclusion and future work

A transparent, modular simulation and optimisation framework was built that satisfies all minimum outputs of Challenge 01 and the Advanced Level requirement. The optimiser raised apogee from 9.28 km to 175.11 km within all resource limits, mainly by using the full propellant allowance, reducing dry mass and drag, launching near-vertically and choosing a higher thrust.

Future work: a pitch / gravity-turn control law, Mach-dependent Cd and ANSYS CFD-derived drag, multi-stage vehicles, structural and g-load constraints, wind and rotating-Earth effects, multiple optimiser seeds, and a multi-objective (altitude vs propellant) Pareto study.
